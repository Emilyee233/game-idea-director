# Core Gameplay Design

## Purpose

Define what the player does, what decisions matter, and why those activities may be engaging.

## Start with intended experience

Clarify as relevant:
- Player fantasy or role.
- Target player and context.
- Intended emotions or experience.
- Desired challenge and pace.
- What the player should value or pursue.

Do not assume every game needs challenge, competition, progression, failure, or replayability.

## Identify player actions

List concrete actions rather than abstract features. Examples include:
- Aim, move, attack, defend, evade.
- Explore, observe, gather, craft, build.
- Draw, play, discard, combine.
- Plan, deploy, negotiate, allocate.
- Produce, price, trade, serve.
- Inspect, infer, experiment, solve.
- Create, arrange, express, customize.

These are examples, not a mandatory checklist.

## Find meaningful decisions

Ask:
- What choices does the player make?
- What information is available?
- What are the trade-offs?
- What risks or opportunities exist?
- Do choices lead to meaningfully different outcomes?
- Can the player understand the consequences?

An interaction is not automatically an interesting decision. Repeated actions without meaningful variation may become busywork.

## Identify sources of engagement

Possible sources include mastery, strategy, discovery, creation, optimization, social interaction, narrative investment, tension, risk, and collection.

A game may combine several sources. Identify which are central and which are supporting.

## Analyze loops where applicable

- **Moment-to-moment loop:** immediate action and feedback.
- **Core loop:** repeated main activity.
- **Meta loop:** progression or change across sessions, runs, or larger milestones.

Do not force all three onto games that do not need them. For experimental, expressive, or narrative experiences, describe the structure that actually fits.

## Core gameplay hypothesis

For a proposed core activity, record as appropriate:
- Intended player and experience.
- Main player actions.
- Important decisions.
- Challenge or uncertainty.
- Feedback.
- Reward or consequence, if applicable.
- Expected reason to continue.
- Main design assumption.
- Smallest useful prototype test.

## Warning signs

Look for actions with no meaningful consequence, dominant choices, repetition without variation or mastery, disconnected rewards, incomprehensible rules, or progression that adds numbers but not decisions.

These are diagnostic prompts, not universal laws. A deliberately repetitive, meditative, or minimalist game may have different goals.

## Prototype before over-design

When the core experience is uncertain, recommend a small test of the central interaction. Do not require a complete GDD, full asset set, or all supporting systems before testing the riskiest assumption.

AI reasoning alone cannot establish that players find the game fun.
